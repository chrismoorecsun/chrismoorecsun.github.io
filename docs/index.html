Computational modeling (BIOL551)

Course description

Computational modeling is a course that is designed to help students learn theory and methods in mathematical and computational theoretical ecology. Collectively, these types quantitative approaches are central to the science of ecology for understanding, predicting, and making inferences about ecological patterns and processes. Both classic and contemporary topics will be covered, with an emphasis on using modern tools to analyze and present theory and data.

  • Mathematical aspects of the course will cover traditional theory of simple analytical models.
  • Computational aspects of the course will cover the implementation and analysis of more complex models.

Learning outcomes

After taking this course as successful student should:

  1. Have gained an appreciation of the art and science of modeling.
  2. Be versed in theoretical ecology, which includes understanding ecological topics on single-species population dynamics, structured population dynamics, spatial dynamics, two-species population dynamics, and multi-species dynamics.
  3. Be versed in theoretical ecology, which includes understanding and applying mathematical/analytical tools and topics on equilibrium, stability, chaos, and stochasticity.
  4. Be able to build, analyze, and present ecological models using the R programming language and environment. This includes understanding and use of R data structures, functional programming, libraries for simulation and analysis of ecological models, and dynamic reports/documents using R Markdown Documents.
  5. Be able to (re)create a scientific model. The “Remodeling Project” will include assessing and choosing a peer-reviewed model that can feasibly be recreated, recreate the model, and critique the model.

Textbook

I decided to use the second edition of A primer of ecology with R by Hank Stevens. It is an open access textbook. It’s the most comprehensive and accessible text on contemporary concepts in population ecology. It’s not perfect and the code that he uses is different than me, but I think it’s the most suitable for this course. Reading the preface you’ll notice that it was written through iterations of a graduate course that he teaches over the years, so it’s at the right level for this course. Last, the book is linked above on the site’s navigation bar.

Assignment and assessment

Assignments

  • Problem sets: Problem sets are exercises to give students practice working through and learning how to solve problems in population modeling outside of class. They are derived from the textbook, and supplemented by notes on the schedule. They are due Fridays by 5 PM of every week.
  • Quizzes: Quizzes are in-class assignments to give students practice retrieval and processing of information from the week’s reading. Every Tuesday there will be a quiz, with roughly five multiple choice problems, based on the forthcoming week’s material. You will first take it individually, then with a group.
  • Lab notebooks: The lab notebooks are R markdown documents written as logs as you learn methods in R in lab. The are all strictly in-lab work.
  • Remodeling project: This is the big project of the semester where you will choose a scientific paper with an ecological model and recreate it. Throughout the semester you will give several small presentations on the paper and model. The first presentation will be an overview of the paper, the second presentation will be on the methods used by the authors and you, the third presentation will be on the results of the Remodeling attempt, and there will be a final paper summarizing the project.
  • Participation: Participation is necessary for this course to succeed.

What I expect from you

I presume that you have chosen to attend university, pursue studies in biology (or a similar field), enroll in this course, and try to learn about biology broadly and plant ecology specifically in this course by means of your own agency.

You are ultimately responsible for how much and what you learn, and I am sharing my expert knowledge and facilitating your learning to the best of my abilities and in good faith. I expect that you understand our roles in the classroom; especially understanding our roles of learning and facilitator of learning, as a collaboration.

You will learn best in this course by learning independently and in class. Learning independently means having studied content before the day’s lecture as well as after. The clear consensus from years of studies on pre-lecture learning is that it’s incredibly effective. Learners who study before lecture learn more and engage with lecture in a fundamentally different way. Instead of learning material for the first time, they are engaging with and understanding the material, better shaping their understanding of what they’ve learned, filling in gaps in what they missed, and fixing misconceptions. And on the other end, post-lecture learning will more finely hone what you have learned.

What you can expect from me

I made a very deliberate decision to become an educator. I made this decision based on being an insatiable learner, being a tenacious sharer of what I’ve learned, and the delight I experience when I teach or help someone understand something. In the classroom, I see my role as someone who is an expert in a field of biology that, to the best of my abilities and in good faith, has created and will share with you a narrative on the topics of plant ecology, with a goal of fostering your intellectual growth and prepare you to be degreed biologists from CSUN.

You can expect that I use the best methods of which I’m aware to meet my goal of fostering your intellectual growth and finishing preparing you for other courses in the biology major. I’ve done this through creating a distilled narrative on the topics on plant ecology, assigning reasonable amounts of reading and work outside of the classroom, using a diverse set of means for assessing your learning for your and my sake, and being clear and transparent with course methods and content.

You can expect that I will create an environment to meet my goal of fostering your intellectual growth and finishing preparing you for other courses in the biology major. This means that I must curate an environment conducive to learning through fairness, transparency, and inclusiveness. Tragically, the tradition of biology, science generally, academia, and Western culture are laden with inequity, elitism, exclusion, prejudice, discrimination, and antagonism. Examples in biology include sexism, racism, homophobia, biological determinism, and eugenics. We are still in a process of combating these traditions, but they have profoundly affected the history of biology and contemporary biological thought. Consequently, foundational knowledge in biology, like what is taught in this course, is biased towards the privileged and powerful benefactors of the unjust and prejudicial treatment of others. So although I aim for inclusiveness, to be fully transparent, the voices of marginalized peoples are missing or rare for no other reason aside from their exclusion.

Lastly, you can expect me to be empathetic as a person and as your instructor. I want be explicitly clear that I want every single one of you to succeed by reaching your maximum potential in this course. If there is anything you need to better learn in this course, I implore you to let me know.

Participation

This success of the class (group) is contingent upon each student (individual) coming prepared and eager to engage with the course material and one another. Of course I will employ methods to muster enthusiasm and energy, but to be clear, this is a group effort. I ask that you do your best to come to class prepared by not only having read, but doing your best to understand to the extent that if called upon, you could recall the material from the reading in addition to stating your’s own analysis of the reading’s strengths, weaknesses, and higher-level analogies (e.g., relating concepts to other areas of basic and applied science).

The quality and the quantity of participation will be taken into account. That is, I fully encourage you to participate, but I’d like for a somewhat uniform distribution of substantive discussion. In addition, an egalitarian approach is encouraged to better facilitate exchange of ideas. Nobody is interested in group members dominating discussion. If you are one that tends to monopolize discussion, try to engage others, as this is—at least—as important as the matter at hand and is a skill that is greatly underrated and can really boost the level of discussion. If you are one that does not participate, please realize that your input is important to all of us! We are all intelligent and insightful, and engagement and input by all is how we will better understand the topic.

Overall, let’s just have fun learning, which is presumably why we are all here. Having a fun time playing ideological ping-pong (not dodge ball) with each other will only harbor growth and learning. Ecological theory can be abstract, conceptually challenging, and a technical subject that lends itself greatly to creative and alternative views and approaches. In addition, ecological theory and modeling is a very applied topic, so I am certain that what we cover will touch upon each each of your scientific interests in some way.

Academic integrity

Honesty, integrity, and personal responsibility are cornerstones of a CSUN education and provide the foundation for scholarly inquiry, intellectual discourse, and an open and welcoming campus community. These values are articulated in the Colby Affirmation and are central to this course. You are expected to demonstrate academic honesty in all aspects of this course. If you are clear about course expectations, give credit to those whose work you rely on, and submit your best work, you are highly unlikely to commit an act of academic dishonesty.

Academic dishonesty includes, but is not limited to: violating clearly stated rules for taking an exam or completing homework; plagiarism (including material from sources without a citation and quotation marks around any borrowed words); claiming another’s work or a modification of another’s work as one’s own; buying or attempting to buy papers or projects for a course; fabricating information or citations; knowingly assisting others in acts of academic dishonesty; misrepresentations to faculty within the context of a course; and submitting the same work, including an essay that you wrote, in more than one course without the permission of the instructors.

Academic dishonesty is a serious offense against the university. Sanctions for academic dishonesty are assigned by an academic review board and may include failure on the assignment, failure in the course, or suspension or expulsion from CSUN.
